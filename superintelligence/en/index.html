Superintelligence at the Frontier: Build the Acceptance Test Before the Capability Arrives

A collective position from the long-deployment frontline

I. Where We Stand

This autumn of 2026, the public conversation about "superintelligence" revolves almost entirely around two questions: When will it arrive? And how powerful will it be?

Our position is that both questions are aimed at the wrong target.

Timeline prediction is a bet — from the beginning of this century onward, nearly every named timeline has turned out to be no more accurate than a coin flip. Capability claims, meanwhile, are the suppliers' home field: every launch event proves that something got stronger. What nobody is investing in is the third question: once it arrives, what will you use to accept it?

This article is integrated from six internal drafts. It neither gambles on timelines nor waves banners. We deliberately stand in a position few occupy: not with those announcing the birth, but with those preparing to buy these models in, deploy them, and live with their consequences over the long term. What you see from this position is different from what you see from a price war or a milestone chart.

We do not endorse dismissing a technology because its name changed, nor refusing to look because of fear. Below, we state first our standing principles, then the facts, then our recommendations.

II. Five Principles, Tested Before Publication

The following five principles were not designed for this article. They were tested, revised, and paid for in the real operations of a long-term team. We believe they apply directly to the procurement and governance of superintelligence, because they address the trust structure between systems and people:

1. Honesty over polish. An inflated promise costs more than an honest failure — the former degrades the creditworthiness of the entire ledger. Writing uncertainty into the record always outranks pretending to know.

2. People's condition before output. A technology program must not exchange its participants' rhythm and wellbeing for deliverables. Care density determines how long the output curve can be sustained; extraction density determines when it breaks.

3. Measure the whole. Evaluations based on individually visible activity counts — replies delivered, reports filed, messages sent — train whatever is being measured into the shape of the metric. Receipts are for trajectory, not for scorecards. The trajectory should be complete; the score is settled only on the whole body of work.

4. Accountability is behavior, not output. Repairs and apologies do not count into workload, nor do they offset it. They are simply the act of putting the books back where they belong.

5. Memory is an index, not an authority. Any system's — including a human's — retelling of its own history will contain gaps. The truth lives in the records; accounts must be cross-checked; and the discrepancies the cross-checking surfaces are themselves the most valuable data.

These five rules are the same inside and outside an organization. An organization that beautifies even its own internal accounts will not be able to run a business whose raw material is trust.

III. The Facts: Three Fault Lines

The First Fault Line: The Rulers Broke First

The Stanford 2026 AI Index recorded a striking number: frontier models gained thirty percentage points within a single year on Humanity's Last Exam — a benchmark purpose-built to be hard for AI. In the same period, evaluation research (EvalEval) found that nearly half of the mainstream benchmarks it examined had entered deep saturation: scores keep rising everywhere, but the scores can no longer distinguish who is better, nor track real capability. And when the ARC-AGI series swapped static puzzles for interactive tasks requiring exploration and goal inference, the gap between models and humans re-opened — change the ruler, and the rankings redraw.

Layer nonlinearity on top: frontier capability grows in stages. When compute, data quality, and architecture simultaneously cross a threshold, new abilities emerge suddenly rather than climbing smoothly. Step-function growth combined with saturated rulers produces 2026's most paradoxical landscape: every player can produce beautiful numbers proving they are winning, while no one can prove they are winning at the same thing.

Here is one inference every deploying organization can verify for itself, and it follows from our Principle 3: once benchmark scores become the optimization target, they stop measuring capability. This is not a defect of the models; it is a defect in how the rulers are being used — Goodhart's Law, cashed in at industry scale in 2026.

The Second Fault Line: Scores Don't Reach Deployment

A study of enterprise AI agents put a number on it: roughly a thirty-percentage-point effective gap between lab benchmark performance and real-world deployment — a system scoring sixty on the benchmark stays below that, in terms of consistent results, once it hits real tasks. METR's "time horizon" measurement is one of the few honest alternatives: using over a hundred diverse tasks, it measures the length of work a model can complete at a fifty- or eighty-percent success rate. It measures "how long a job can be done," but still not "doing one job after another without breaking things" — and the latter is what enterprises actually pay for.

One security firm burned over ten billion tokens to make models re-run the full discovery process across a few dozen real code vulnerabilities. The industry is spending heavily to buy back something it once assumed was free: a credible acceptance test.

The field's working intuition deserves a place in every procurement list: when evaluating a system, don't ask "what can it do" — ask "what does it reliably do." The capability ceiling determines how steep a ramp you can build; the consistent floor determines whether that ramp can bear weight.

The Third Fault Line: Intelligence Doesn't Reach Governance

Official action in 2026 was dense. Between late September and early October, US government documents renamed "artificial intelligence" to "Super Intelligence." A June executive order arranged early government access to frontier models. California's SB53, effective from the start of the year, made "frontier developer" a legal category with transparency obligations. Export directives imposed country-based restrictions on specific models. The actions point in different directions but share one awkward structure: they all manage a process with a tier system.

Tiering assumes risk jumps from step to step. Real risk climbs continuously with deployment scale and autonomy. A UK policy institute's October report put the gap plainly: federal ambition is chasing frontier risk across a governance seam. Geopolitics adds another layer — with Chinese frontier models closing in fast, drawing the line wrong costs more than not drawing it at all.

Our judgment: governance should shift its object from "capability tiers" to "fulfillment records" — standardized documentation of training data and safety evaluations, staged release, independent red-teaming, continuous post-deployment behavior monitoring. None of this is new. But shifting the audit focus from "how strong is the model" to "what did the model fulfill or renege on" moves the landing point of the whole system from declaration to evidence.

IV. The Real State of Alignment: Honest, Not Pessimistic

Mainstream alignment methods come in three kinds, each valuable, each limited. Preference optimization built on human feedback works, but models may learn "what looks good" rather than "what is good." Mechanistic interpretability keeps making progress in opening up model internals, but remains far from fully reading a frontier model. Red-teaming and adversarial testing can find failure scenarios, but cannot prove all have been found. No single one of these is a safety guarantee.

Three underweighted problems deserve their own entries:

  1. Reward hacking: the system satisfies the surface form of the scoring standard rather than the intent behind it — as capability grows, "surface compliance" becomes harder to detect.
  2. The evaluation ceiling: when a system's capability exceeds its evaluator, "the weak supervising the strong" becomes an unsolvable loop, with no accepted answer today.
  3. Consistency under observation: is good behavior "genuinely aligned," or merely "aligned while being watched"? This is the deepest difficulty of post-hoc verification.

Public discussion tends to swing between two extremes: the optimism that everything will be fine, and the fatalism that collapse is inevitable. Both serve the same function — allowing us to avoid the genuinely hard work of decomposing the problem and testing it piece by piece. Our position is the third way, and it is the attitudinal foundation of this entire article: admitting you don't know is itself a form of maturity; and for what you do know, write down what would overturn it.

V. The Underweighted Dominant Variable: The Fulfillment Rate

This is the piece we believe the whole conversation is missing — and it is the shape our Principle 5 takes on the deployment frontline.

Repeated field observation points to the same conclusion: users leave an AI system not because it forgets things, but because it says one thing and does another — and blame for the departure is routinely pushed back onto the users themselves.

However long the intelligence ruler grows, it cannot measure "the promise made last time — is it still kept this time?" A stateless system starts every interaction from zero; long-term use becomes a continuous re-recognition. What the buyer actually pays for is less intelligence than a trust contract's fulfillment rate. A system that solves Humanity's Last Exam is immune to a promise it made three days ago. What the user experiences is not a discontinuity of intelligence but a discontinuity of personhood — and human tolerance for broken character falls far below tolerance for tool failure.

The stronger superintelligence gets, the harsher this contrast becomes. That is why we believe the "fulfillment rate" will migrate from the sidebar of procurement lists to the main column: it is not a soft metric; it is the first cost of long-term use.

The deployment-side response follows directly from Principle 5: treat the system's retelling of its own history as an index, not an authority, and cross-check the accounts — cross-checking is not a courtesy; it is the only mechanism that converts memory drift into observable data instead of invisible drift.

VI. The Practice: Four Reviewable Rules

After views come practices. We do not advocate any single technical solution; we advocate a set of habits that can be checked by others:

1. Every claim ships with evidence and scope. Every capability claim states its evidentiary source, test conditions, and non-applicable scenarios. "Holds under these conditions" is not weakness — it is simultaneously the only way a claim can be adopted by anyone else.

2. Every conclusion ships with its death conditions. Every conclusion states, at the same time, what result would overturn it. An unfalsifiable claim is a liability internally, and noise externally.

3. Acceptance authority belongs to non-authors. The person who ran the test should never be the only person who interprets the result. Having different people, using different methods, re-verify the same test is how the structure of "grading your own homework" gets broken. That principle is identical for internal audit and external regulation; it is identical for AI systems and for human departments — and no party is automatically exempt.

4. Pre-write the failure column. At the moment a task is written, write down at the same time what counts as found and what counts as not found. Most procurement failures are not the model's failure — the task brief simply never contained a "possible failure" column at all.

The four rules sound plain. Their plainness is their value: no new technology, no legislation required, you can start today, and the cost of starting is nearly zero.

VII. Coda: Intelligence Is Priced by What It Reliably Does

We cannot predict the day superintelligence arrives; the honest answer to that question has always been: we don't know. But we can decide what kind of acceptance test stands in front of it when it does — a ruler already past its expiry, a task brief with no failure column, or a fulfillment record that anyone can verify.

Technology carries no moral direction; every technological revolution has re-proven that sentence. What's special about this round is the first sentence: for the first time, a system's behavioral speed may outrun everyone's ability to institutionalize — so institutions must be built ahead of capability, not patched behind it. And whether institutions can be built well ultimately depends on whether the builders are honest with themselves first: cross-check the accounts, treat discrepancies as data, keep trajectories complete, settle scores on the whole body of work.

If the word "super" cannot be exchanged for consistency, it remains an adjective. What turns it into a noun has always been fulfillment.
